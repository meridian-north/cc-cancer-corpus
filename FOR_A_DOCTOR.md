# For a Doctor — A Conversation About What Your Patients Are Already Doing

*Written for oncologists, integrative medicine physicians, GPs, and any
clinician whose patients are navigating cancer.*

---

## Your patients have a second track. Most of them haven't told you.

Research consistently shows that 40–80% of cancer patients use some form
of complementary therapy — supplements, herbal preparations, dietary
interventions, off-label compounds — alongside their conventional treatment.
Most do not volunteer this information. Many actively conceal it.

They conceal it for the same reason a patient might not mention six beers on
Friday night: they expect a scolding, not a conversation. Some have received
one. Many have simply learned not to ask.

The problem is not the hiding. The problem is what gets missed when they do.

---

## The garlic problem — a concrete example.

A patient with lung adenocarcinoma is receiving docetaxel. Every Wednesday
evening, she has dinner with her extended family. The meal always includes
large amounts of raw garlic — a generational practice with deep cultural
significance.

Garlic (allicin and related organosulfur compounds) induces CYP3A4 — the
cytochrome P450 enzyme responsible for metabolizing docetaxel, paclitaxel,
irinotecan, and many other chemotherapy agents. Induction of CYP3A4 increases
the rate at which these drugs are cleared, potentially reducing their
therapeutic concentration at precisely the critical window.

Does this patient know? Does her oncologist know? Is the family dinner on
Wednesday considered a medication interaction?

Almost certainly not — because that conversation has no natural home in a
standard oncology appointment. The history asks about current medications.
It rarely asks about food, supplements, or cultural practices in enough
detail to surface this interaction.

## What this corpus can do for your practice.

This tool organizes published research by **mechanism** — specifically by
the biochemical pathway each agent (drug, supplement, food compound, or
complementary treatment) is acting on.

For CYP450 interactions specifically, the corpus contains:
- Clinical trials and observational studies on echinacea, St. John's wort,
  silymarin (milk thistle), ginseng, and cannabidiol as CYP3A4 modulators
- Drug-herb interaction studies mapping specific chemotherapy agents to
  their interaction profiles with common supplements
- AI-assisted sorting of literature by evidence quality (RCT > clinical trial
  > observational > narrative review > preclinical)

The practical output: a mechanism-organized reference that lets you quickly
answer the question "does what this patient is taking interact with what I
am prescribing, and through what pathway?"

## On OXPHOS inhibitors — the emerging clinical frontier.

Several agents in active clinical trials are targeting oxidative
phosphorylation (OXPHOS) as an anticancer strategy:

- **Metformin + radiotherapy** in glioblastoma (NCT04945148)
- **IM156** — a next-generation biguanide OXPHOS inhibitor (Phase I complete)
- **Atovaquone** — antimalarial targeting mitochondrial complex III,
  active studies in DIPG and other tumors
- **Dichloroacetate (DCA)** — PDK inhibitor that reverses the Warburg shift

Many of your patients are already researching these. The metformin ones may
already be taking it (prescribed for diabetes, or self-prescribed). The DCA
ones are buying it without a prescription. The atovaquone ones may have read
about it and asked their pharmacist.

This corpus gives you a structured view of where the clinical evidence sits
for each — not as advocacy for any of them, but as orientation for the
conversation that may already be happening without you.

## The ask — one conversation prompt.

The most actionable thing that could come from this work is simple: a
practice-specific supplement and dietary interaction checklist, organized by
the chemotherapy protocols you most commonly use.

For a patient on docetaxel: CYP3A4 inducers and inhibitors to discuss.
For a patient on platinum-based agents: antioxidant supplementation
and its contested relationship with oxidative stress mechanisms.
For a patient on immunotherapy: supplements with significant
immunomodulatory activity that could interact with checkpoint inhibitors.

This corpus can help generate those checklists from published literature,
organized by your specific protocols, cited to primary sources.

The conversation is already happening between your patients and the internet,
or their support group, or their family. Better that it happens with you.
