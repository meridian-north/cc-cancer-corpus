# For a Stranger — What This Is

*Plain language. No medical background assumed.*

---

## Cancer cells are unusually hungry.

Normal cells in your body produce energy by breaking down glucose in the
presence of oxygen — an efficient process. Cancer cells often do something
strange: they prefer a much less efficient energy pathway even when oxygen
is available. They consume glucose at far higher rates, and they can
survive in conditions that would kill most normal cells.

This quirk was noticed by a German scientist named Otto Warburg in 1924.
It is called the Warburg effect. For decades, researchers have asked
whether this energy dependence could be exploited — whether cutting off
or disrupting the cancer cell's energy supply might be a treatment strategy.

---

## Some of the most promising research is happening at this intersection.

Drugs originally developed for other purposes — metformin (a diabetes
medication), atovaquone (an antimalarial) — have been found to interfere
with the energy machinery that cancer cells depend on. Several clinical
trials are testing these and similar drugs specifically as anticancer
agents, often combined with standard chemotherapy or radiation.

At the same time, a large body of research has accumulated on plant-based
compounds, dietary patterns, and supplements that interact with the same
cellular machinery. Some of this research is rigorous. Some is preliminary.
Some is contested. The problem is that all of it lives in different journals,
different databases, and different communities — and almost none of it is
organized in a way that a patient or their doctor can actually use.

---

## This tool organizes that research by one question: how does it work?

It does not say what to take or not take. It does not rank treatments.
It does not have opinions about conventional versus alternative medicine.

What it does is place every treatment — from a chemotherapy drug to a cup of
green tea — on a map organized by biological mechanism. A chemotherapy drug
that blocks tumor blood supply and a plant compound that blocks the same
pathway in the same way end up at the same address on the map. Not because
they are equivalent, but because they share a mechanism — and that shared
mechanism is what matters for understanding interactions, for asking
research questions, and for having informed conversations.

---

## What it cannot do, stated plainly.

It cannot tell you whether any treatment will help you.
It cannot replace your oncologist, your support group, or the community
of people around you who know your situation.
It cannot resolve the fundamental problem of cancer research: most of what
works in a lab does not work the same way in a human body, and most of
what works in one cancer does not work the same way in another.

What it can do is give you and your care team better questions to ask.

---

## If you are navigating cancer — a word from the person who built this.

I meet with cancer patients every week. I have an oncologist myself.
I know that the space between "what my doctor told me" and "what I read
last night at 2 a.m." can feel very large and very lonely.

The most useful single piece of advice I can offer: **do not do this alone.**

There are official cancer support groups — covered by insurance, staffed by
professionals, connected to clinical infrastructure. There are unofficial ones,
patient-led, paid for by patients, run by people who have been where you are.
Both kinds matter. The official ones have expertise. The unofficial ones have
experience and the willingness to say things that nobody else will say.

Neither replaces your oncologist. But both can help you arrive at that
appointment with better questions, more context, and less isolation.

This tool is one part of that. It is a map. The journey is still yours.
