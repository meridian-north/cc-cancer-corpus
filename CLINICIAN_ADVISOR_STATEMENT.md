# A note on using this tool — from the person who built it

*I am not a clinician. I am a patient advocate and independent researcher
who meets with cancer patients weekly, and who has an oncologist myself.
What follows is what I was advised, what I have observed, and the honest
cautions I want to pass along.*

---

## What my clinician has told me

Tell your doctor what you are taking. All of it. Not just your prescriptions —
your supplements, your teas, the things your family has always eaten, the
protocols you found in a support group at 2 a.m.

The conversation does not have to be an apology. It can be: "I want you to
know what I'm doing so you can tell me if anything conflicts with my treatment."
That is a clinically useful statement. It protects you.

The reason clinicians may not ask about these things is not that they don't
matter — it is that standard intake forms are not designed to surface them.
Your oncologist may not know that garlic induces the enzyme that processes your
chemotherapy. That is not a failure of care; it is a gap in the system. This
tool exists to help close that gap.

My clinician's advice, which I pass on: **do not make treatment changes based
on anything you read online, including this. Bring it to a person who knows
your case.**

---

## What I have observed meeting with cancer patients

The people who do best at navigating this space tend to have a few things
in common:

They are transparent with their care team, even when they expect pushback.
They bring questions, not conclusions. They do not start or stop anything
significant without discussing it first.

They use support groups — both official ones attached to cancer centers,
and unofficial patient-led communities — as a place to process information
before acting on it. These communities provide a kind of human consensus
that no database can.

They understand that being informed is not the same as being in charge of
their own pharmacology.

## Cautions from the AI tools used to build this corpus

*These were generated in collaboration with Claude (Anthropic), which was
used extensively to build and refine this corpus. They reflect honest
limitations of AI-assisted health research.*

**What this tool does well:**
- Organizing published literature by mechanism
- Identifying where drug-herb interactions have been formally studied
- Summarizing evidence levels (clinical trial vs. observational vs. lab only)
- Helping you formulate better questions for your oncologist

**What this tool cannot do:**
- Account for your specific cancer type, stage, and treatment protocol
- Know your current medications and how they interact with each other
- Replace a pharmacist's review of your full medication list
- Know whether a study in one cancer type applies to yours
- Assess your current organ function, which affects how your body processes drugs

**Where AI assistance in health research has known risks:**
- AI systems can present information confidently that is outdated or misapplied
- Mechanisms that work in a laboratory may not translate to clinical outcomes
- AI cannot perform a clinical interview or physical examination
- The gap between "plausible mechanism" and "proven in humans" is large and
  matters enormously when you are making treatment decisions

**The specific caution I would emphasize:** the interaction table in this repository
is a starting point for conversation with your oncologist or pharmacist, not a
complete picture of your risk. Drug interactions depend on dose, timing, individual
metabolism, and other medications. A clinical pharmacist with your full medication
list is more valuable than any database.
