# Drug-Herb-Food Interaction Table — Expanded
## For Cancer Patients and Their Oncologists

*Built from published literature. Evidence level noted for each entry.*
*This is a starting point for conversation with your care team, not a complete guide.*
*Last expanded: 2026-06-05*

**Evidence levels:**
- Clinical trial / RCT = highest (tested in controlled human studies)
- Observational = moderate (observed in humans, not controlled)
- In vitro / preclinical = lowest (lab only — may not apply in humans)

## Section 1 — CYP3A4 (the most important enzyme for chemotherapy)

Drugs metabolized by CYP3A4 include: docetaxel, paclitaxel, irinotecan,
vincristine, vinblastine, cyclophosphamide, etoposide, imatinib (Gleevec),
erlotinib, gefitinib, lapatinib, everolimus, temsirolimus, tamoxifen (partial),
many targeted therapies and immunosuppressants.

### Inducers (speed up drug clearance — may reduce effectiveness)

| Substance | Strength | Notes | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| St. John's Wort | Strong | Most significant herbal CYP3A4 inducer; can halve drug levels | Clinical trial |
| Garlic (high-dose supplement) | Moderate | Culinary use less concerning; supplements around infusion days | Observational |
| Echinacea purpurea | Moderate | Clinical trial directly with docetaxel showing reduced blood levels | Clinical trial |
| Ginseng (Panax) | Moderate | Pharmacokinetic studies confirm interaction | Clinical trial |
| Kava (Piper methysticum) | Moderate | Less studied; also hepatotoxic | Observational |
| Black cohosh (Cimicifuga) | Mild–moderate | Commonly used for menopausal symptoms in cancer patients | Observational |
| Valerian | Mild | Sedative herb; CYP inducer at higher doses | In vitro / observational |
| Schisandra (wu wei zi) | Moderate | Traditional Chinese medicine; well-documented inducer | Observational |
| Cruciferous vegetables (sulforaphane) | Mild | Broccoli, Brussels sprouts at very high intake | Observational |
| Charbroiled / smoked meats | Mild | Heterocyclic amines induce CYP1A2 and CYP3A4 | Observational |
| Tobacco smoke | Moderate | Active smoking induces CYPs; affects many drugs | Clinical |

### Inhibitors (slow drug clearance — may increase side effects or toxicity)

| Substance | Strength | Notes | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Grapefruit juice | Strong | Furanocoumarins block CYP3A4; avoid entirely with many drugs | Clinical trial |
| Seville orange (bitter orange) | Strong | Same mechanism as grapefruit; often in marmalade | Clinical |
| Pomegranate juice | Moderate | CYP3A4 inhibitor; may affect similar drugs to grapefruit | Observational |
| CBD (cannabidiol) | Moderate–strong | Broad drug interaction profile; clinical trials confirm | Clinical trial |
| Goldenseal (berberine) | Moderate | Strong CYP3A4 inhibitor; also CYP2D6 | Clinical trial |
| Quercetin | Moderate | Found in many foods; stronger at supplement doses | In vitro / observational |
| Turmeric/curcumin | Mild–moderate | Common supplement; interaction depends on dose and form | In vitro |
| Silymarin (milk thistle) | Mild | Effect confirmed; clinical significance varies | Clinical trial |
| Resveratrol | Mild | Red wine source; more significant at supplement doses | In vitro |
| Black pepper (piperine) | Moderate | Often combined with turmeric; significantly increases absorption of multiple drugs including some chemotherapy | Observational |
| Watercress | Mild | CYP2E1 and CYP3A4; relevant for cisplatin and others | Observational |
| Green tea (EGCG) | Mild | Multiple CYP interactions | Observational |

## Section 2 — CYP2D6 (CRITICAL for tamoxifen — breast cancer patients read this)

Tamoxifen requires CYP2D6 to convert to its active cancer-fighting form (endoxifen).
**Inhibitors of CYP2D6 can make tamoxifen essentially ineffective.**

This is one of the most clinically significant and underrecognized interactions in oncology.
Many breast cancer patients are simultaneously prescribed antidepressants that block CYP2D6.

### CYP2D6 inhibitors (particularly relevant with tamoxifen)

| Substance | Effect | Notes | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fluoxetine (Prozac) | Strong inhibitor | Commonly prescribed for hot flashes in breast cancer patients; significantly reduces tamoxifen activation | Clinical trial |
| Paroxetine (Paxil) | Strong inhibitor | Similar to fluoxetine; ask your oncologist about alternatives (venlafaxine, citalopram have less CYP2D6 effect) | Clinical trial |
| Bupropion | Moderate inhibitor | Sometimes prescribed for depression; discuss with oncologist | Clinical |
| Goldenseal (berberine) | Moderate inhibitor | Also inhibits CYP3A4; double interaction concern | Clinical trial |
| Black cohosh | Mild inhibitor | Commonly used for menopausal symptoms in breast cancer patients on tamoxifen | Observational |
| Cimetidine (Tagamet) | Mild inhibitor | OTC heartburn medication | Clinical |
| Quinidine | Strong inhibitor | Antiarrhythmic drug | Clinical |

**For tamoxifen patients specifically:** ask your oncologist which antidepressant is safest. This is a documented, treatable interaction with a major impact on cancer outcomes.

## Section 5 — P-glycoprotein (P-gp) drug transporter

P-gp is an efflux pump in cancer cells and the gut. It pumps drugs OUT of cells.
P-gp inducers can reduce drug accumulation inside cancer cells.
P-gp inhibitors can increase drug levels (potentially useful OR toxic).

Drugs affected: paclitaxel, docetaxel, vincristine, vinblastine, irinotecan,
doxorubicin, etoposide, imatinib, several targeted therapies.

| Substance | Effect on P-gp | Clinical implication | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| St. John's Wort | Strong inducer | Reduces drug accumulation in cells; reduces efficacy | Clinical trial |
| Quercetin | Inhibitor | May increase drug levels; not always beneficial | In vitro / observational |
| Curcumin/turmeric | Inhibitor | Some evidence of P-gp modulation | In vitro |
| Resveratrol | Inhibitor | May increase intracellular drug concentration | In vitro |
| Silymarin (milk thistle) | Inhibitor | Multiple transporter effects | In vitro |
| Piperine (black pepper) | Inhibitor | Dramatically increases absorption of many co-administered compounds | Observational |
| EGCG (green tea) | Modulator | Complex effects on multiple transporters | In vitro |
| Vitamin E TPGS | Inhibitor | Vitamin E form used in some formulations | In vitro |

## Section 6 — OATP transporters (drug uptake into liver/cells)

OATP1B1 and OATP1B2 are important for drug uptake into the liver and some
targeted cells. Inhibitors can reduce drug clearance and increase toxicity.

| Substance | Effect | Drugs affected | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Grapefruit juice | Inhibitor | Multiple statins, some cancer drugs | Clinical trial |
| Apple juice | Inhibitor | OATP1A2; affects some drugs | Clinical |
| Orange juice | Inhibitor | OATP1A2; affects fexofenadine, others | Clinical |
| Green tea | Inhibitor | OATP1A2 | In vitro |
| Quercetin | Inhibitor | Multiple OATP subtypes | In vitro |
| Rifampin | Inducer | Antibacterial; major transporter inducer | Clinical |

## Section 7 — Antioxidant interactions with oxidative-stress chemotherapy

Some chemotherapy works via oxidative mechanisms. High-dose antioxidants are
debated — some research suggests they may protect cancer cells as well as healthy cells.
The evidence is contested. The clinical significance is unclear and likely depends on
specific drug + specific antioxidant + cancer type.

**Discuss explicitly with your oncologist before taking these during active chemotherapy.**

| Substance | Concern | Drugs of particular concern | Evidence status |
|---|---|---|---|
| High-dose Vitamin C (IV) | May reduce oxidative mechanism | Platinum agents (cisplatin, carboplatin), anthracyclines | Actively debated; some trials suggest benefit, some concern |
| High-dose Vitamin E | Antioxidant interference debated | Alkylating agents | Narrative review; contested |
| N-acetylcysteine (NAC) | Glutathione precursor; antioxidant | Alkylating agents; platinum | Preclinical concern; limited clinical data |
| Alpha-lipoic acid | Mitochondrial antioxidant | Multiple | Preclinical; human data limited |
| CoQ10 | Mitochondrial cofactor | Doxorubicin (may protect heart AND cancer cells) | Small trials; use with caution |
| Selenium | Antioxidant at high dose | Cisplatin and others | Contested; may also sensitize |
| Beta-carotene | Pro-oxidant at high dose in smokers | Multiple | Lung cancer risk increase documented in smokers |

**Note:** Oral Vitamin C at nutritional doses (up to 1-2g/day) is different from IV high-dose
Vitamin C (25-75g infusions). The interaction concern is primarily with very high doses.

## Section 8 — Immunotherapy interactions (checkpoint inhibitors)

Checkpoint inhibitors (pembrolizumab/Keytruda, nivolumab/Opdivo, ipilimumab/Yervoy,
atezolizumab, durvalumab, and others) work by activating the immune system.
Substances that significantly modulate immune function may interact unpredictably
— in either direction.

| Substance | Concern | Notes | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Echinacea | Immune stimulation | Theoretical interaction with checkpoint inhibitors; case reports | Observational |
| Medicinal mushrooms (PSK/turkey tail, reishi, shiitake) | Immune modulation | Under active study as immunotherapy adjuncts; may enhance or confuse | Early clinical |
| Melatonin | Immune modulator | Complex immunological effects | Small trials |
| CBD / THC | Immunomodulatory | Emerging evidence; complex profile | Early clinical |
| High-dose Vitamin D | Immunomodulatory | At pharmacological (not dietary) doses | Observational |
| Astragalus | Immune stimulant | Traditional Chinese medicine; immune effects documented | In vitro / observational |
| Corticosteroids | Immune suppressor | Commonly used to manage checkpoint inhibitor side effects; reduces efficacy | Clinical |

## Section 12 — Anticoagulant interactions (bleeding risk)

Many cancer patients take anticoagulants. Multiple herbs affect bleeding risk.
This section is specifically for those on warfarin, heparin, or newer anticoagulants.

| Substance | Effect on bleeding | Drugs of concern | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Garlic (high dose) | Increases bleeding risk | Warfarin, heparin | Clinical |
| Ginkgo biloba | Increases bleeding risk | Warfarin, aspirin | Clinical trial |
| Ginger (high dose) | Mild antiplatelet | Warfarin | Observational |
| Fish oil / omega-3 (high dose) | Antiplatelet | Warfarin, antiplatelet drugs | Clinical |
| Vitamin E (high dose) | Anticoagulant effect | Warfarin | Clinical |
| Feverfew | Antiplatelet | Warfarin, aspirin | Observational |
| Dong quai | Coumarin-containing | Warfarin | Observational |

## The single most important message

Tell your oncologist and your pharmacist everything you take. Every supplement,
every herb, every tea, every high-dose vitamin. Not as a confession — as
clinical information that protects you.

The gap this table addresses is not your secrecy. It is the fact that most
oncologists do not have this list available in a form they can quickly check
against your treatment protocol. This table is a starting point. A pharmacist
with your full list is the destination.
