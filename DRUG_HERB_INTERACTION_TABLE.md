# Drug-Herb-Food Interaction Table
## For Cancer Patients and Their Oncologists

*Generated from published literature in this corpus. Evidence level noted for
each entry. This is hypothesis-generating, not prescriptive. Discuss with
your oncologist before changing anything.*

**How to use this table:**
- Find your chemotherapy drug in the "Drugs affected" column
- Check what substances interact with it and how
- Bring the relevant rows to your next oncology appointment as questions

**Evidence levels used:**
- RCT / clinical trial = highest (tested in humans, controlled)
- Observational = moderate (observed in humans, not controlled)
- In vitro / preclinical = lowest (lab only — may not apply to humans)

## Section 1 — CYP450 Enzyme Interactions
*These interactions affect how quickly your body processes chemotherapy drugs.
An "inducer" speeds up drug clearance (potentially reducing effectiveness).
An "inhibitor" slows clearance (potentially increasing side effects).*

### CYP3A4 — the most important enzyme for chemotherapy metabolism

**Drugs metabolized by CYP3A4 (partial list):**
Docetaxel, paclitaxel, irinotecan, vincristine, vinblastine, imatinib (Gleevec),
erlotinib, gefitinib, tamoxifen, cyclophosphamide, etoposide, many others.

| Substance | Effect on CYP3A4 | Clinical significance | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| St. John's Wort | Strong inducer | Significantly reduces blood levels of many chemo drugs | Clinical trial |
| Garlic (high dose) | Inducer | May reduce docetaxel, irinotecan effectiveness | Observational |
| Echinacea | Inducer (significant) | Studied directly with docetaxel — reduced drug levels | Clinical trial |
| Ginseng (Panax) | Modulator | Pharmacokinetic interaction confirmed in trials | Clinical trial |
| Grapefruit juice | Strong inhibitor | Increases drug levels — can raise toxicity | Clinical trial |
| CBD (cannabidiol) | Inhibitor | Clinically significant — broad drug interaction profile | Clinical trial |
| Milk thistle (silymarin) | Mild inhibitor | Effect confirmed; clinical significance varies by drug | Clinical trial |
| Green tea (EGCG) | Mild modulator | Less consistent than above; still discuss with oncologist | Observational |

### CYP1A2

| Substance | Effect | Drugs affected | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Quercetin | Inhibitor | Colorectal cancer drugs; tamoxifen | International lit. |
| Cruciferous vegetables (sulforaphane) | Inducer | Multiple | Observational |
| Grapefruit | Inhibitor | Erlotinib, others | Clinical trial |

### CYP2C9

| Substance | Effect | Drugs affected | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|

## Section 2 — Antioxidant interactions with oxidative-stress-based chemotherapy

*Some chemotherapy drugs work by generating oxidative stress to kill cancer cells.
High-dose antioxidants may theoretically reduce their effectiveness.
The evidence is contested — some researchers argue antioxidants protect healthy cells
without protecting cancer cells. Discuss this specifically with your oncologist.*

| Substance | Interaction concern | Drugs of concern | Evidence status |
|---|---|---|---|
| High-dose Vitamin C (IV) | May reduce oxidative stress mechanism | Platinum agents, anthracyclines | Contested; active research |
| High-dose Vitamin E | Antioxidant interference debated | Multiple | Narrative review; contested |
| N-acetylcysteine (NAC) | Antioxidant; glutathione precursor | Alkylating agents | In vitro / preclinical |
| Alpha-lipoic acid | Antioxidant; mitochondrial effects | Multiple | Preclinical |
| CoQ10 | Mitochondrial cofactor / antioxidant | Doxorubicin (may protect heart) | Small trials; observe |

**Note:** High-dose Vitamin C *given intravenously* has different pharmacology than
oral Vitamin C. Some oncologists use it; others avoid it. This is an active debate,
not a settled question.

## Section 3 — Immunomodulatory substances and immunotherapy

*Checkpoint inhibitors (pembrolizumab, nivolumab, ipilimumab, etc.) work by
activating the immune system. Substances that significantly modulate immune
function may interact unpredictably.*

| Substance | Concern | Notes | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Echinacea | Immune stimulation | Theoretical interaction with checkpoint inhibitors | Observational |
| Medicinal mushrooms (PSK/turkey tail, reishi) | Immune modulation | Under active study as immunotherapy adjuncts | Clinical trials pending |
| Melatonin | Immune modulator | Some evidence of interaction with immune response | Small trials |
| CBD / THC | Immunomodulatory | Under investigation; complex profile | Early clinical |

## Section 4 — Metabolic interactions (energy metabolism / OXPHOS node)

*Several chemotherapy protocols and emerging agents target cancer cell energy
metabolism. Some supplements operate on the same pathways.*

| Substance | Mechanism | Relevant drug interactions | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Metformin (if co-prescribed for diabetes) | OXPHOS inhibitor | Additive effect with OXPHOS-targeting chemo; also enhances some immunotherapy | Clinical trial |
| Quercetin | Mitochondrial / CYP1A2 | Multiple; especially colorectal agents | International lit. |
| Artemisinin / artesunate | Mitochondrial redox | Some chemo drugs; discuss with oncologist | Observational |
| Dichloroacetate (DCA) | PDK inhibitor, reverses Warburg effect | Experimental context; interacts with glucose metabolism | Preclinical + early clinical |

## Section 5 — The garlic example in detail

*Because this comes up often and illustrates the hidden interaction problem.*

**Garlic** (Allium sativum) contains organosulfur compounds (allicin and related)
that induce CYP3A4 — the enzyme responsible for metabolizing many chemotherapy drugs.

Clinical significance depends on:
- Amount consumed (culinary use vs. high-dose supplement are different)
- Which specific chemo drug (docetaxel and irinotecan are most sensitive)
- Frequency and timing relative to infusion

**The honest answer:** Culinary garlic in normal amounts is probably fine.
High-dose garlic supplements taken around chemo infusion days are worth discussing.
Garlic as a cultural / dietary practice that your family has always done
deserves a real conversation with your oncologist, not a dismissal or a lecture.

If your oncologist says "avoid garlic" without asking how much, how often, or
why it's part of your diet, that's a signal that more conversation is needed.

## What this table does not contain

- A complete list of all drug-herb interactions (this is not that; it is a starting point)
- Efficacy claims for any supplement
- Dosing recommendations for anything
- Instructions to start or stop any substance

Every entry here comes with the same caveat: this is published research organized
by mechanism and evidence level. Your oncologist, pharmacist, or integrative
medicine specialist can help you interpret what's relevant to your specific
treatment protocol.
